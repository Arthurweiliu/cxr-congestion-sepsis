# Discordance between radiographic congestion and the charted fluid balance in sepsis: a cohort study of 3,944 patients

Wei Liu 1,*, Dongqing Ma 2, Jian Jiang 1, Xuefeng Xu 1, Rongjuan Zeng 1, Aoqiong Li 1 and Bo Zhu 1,*

1 Department of General Intensive Care Unit, Beijing Anzhen Hospital, Capital Medical University, Beijing, China
2 Department of Pediatrics, Civil Aviation General Hospital, Beijing, China

\* Correspondence: liuwei2153@126.com; zhubo123123@sina.com

---

**Abstract**

**Background.** In sepsis, the admission radiograph and the fluid chart are often read as describing the same process, although they do not. We quantified their agreement, the prognosis of each, and treatment given after the film.

**Methods.** Retrospective cohort of 3,944 adults with Sepsis-3 in MIMIC-IV. Congestion was classified from radiology reports; balance was charted intake minus output from the later of film and ICU admission to 72 h after the film, normalised to weight.

**Results.** Agreement was below chance (κ = −0.11); 493 patients (12.5%) had congestion with a non-positive balance. Mortality was 10.5%, 20.0%, 18.3% and 32.8%, with adjusted odds ratios of 1.90 (95% confidence interval [CI] 1.42–2.56), 1.75 (1.22–2.52) and 3.27 (2.39–4.48). The discordant estimate ranged 1.40–2.64 across ten sensitivity analyses: 1.40 (0.87–2.25) in the 62.5% of patients whose window was truncated and 2.27 (1.27–4.04) where it was not. Adding congestion to a model with a balance spline raised the AUC from 0.732 to 0.740 (ΔAUC 0.0083, 95% CI 0.0015–0.0159). Post hoc, loop diuretics followed the film in 68.8% of group C versus 37.4% of group A.

**Conclusions.** A congested film and the subsequent charted balance agreed less often than chance, consistent with treatment given after the film. Congestion carried prognostic information beyond case mix but added little discrimination, and neither its independence from the magnitude of the balance nor its excess risk in the 62.5% with a truncated window was established. These findings apply to patients with a classifiable report and a calculable balance.

**Keywords:** sepsis; pulmonary oedema; fluid balance; chest radiograph; MIMIC-IV; cohort study; agreement

## 1. Introduction

Sepsis is characterised by vasodilation, endothelial injury and capillary leak. Resuscitation commonly produces a positive fluid balance [1,2], and randomised comparisons of restrictive with liberal strategies have not demonstrated a mortality benefit [3–5]. Accumulated fluid is consistently associated with organ dysfunction and death [6–8], but the effect size is modest; it varies with admission haemoglobin [9], ventilatory settings [10] and haemodynamic phenotype [11], and is difficult to separate from the severity of illness that drives both the fluid given and the outcome.

Alongside the fluid chart, the chest radiograph is one of the most readily available assessments of pulmonary congestion in the intensive care unit. Radiographic pulmonary oedema, pleural effusion and vascular engorgement are reported within hours of admission, and unlike lung ultrasound they require no additional bedside operator skill. The radiograph and the chart are commonly read together as though they described the same process: fluid goes in, fluid accumulates, the lungs become wet.

That equation is questionable. Pulmonary oedema in sepsis is frequently due to increased capillary permeability rather than hydrostatic pressure, and may develop at a normal or even negative cumulative balance. Conversely, a positive balance may be tolerated without radiographic change when the right heart and lymphatic drainage are intact. Two small studies have probed this discrepancy without resolving it. A cross-sectional study of 50 critically ill patients found that cumulative fluid balance correlated with neither clinical signs of oedema nor a comprehensive ultrasound assessment including lung ultrasound and the venous excess ultrasound score [12,13]. A retrospective study of 102 patients with septic acute kidney injury found that a composite definition of cardiopulmonary congestion — incorporating clinical, radiographic, echocardiographic and biomarker criteria — was present in 78.4% of patients yet was not associated with in-hospital survival, dialysis requirement or renal recovery [14]. Both were small, single-centre, and neither reported multivariable adjustment.

We therefore asked three questions in a large cohort. First, how often do radiographic congestion and measured fluid balance disagree? Second, does radiographic congestion carry prognostic information beyond the direction of the fluid balance and beyond shock severity? Third, does it improve risk prediction beyond routinely available variables? We deliberately framed the third question as one that could be answered negatively, because a widely available test that adds nothing should be identified as such.

## 2. Materials and Methods

### 2.1. Study design and data source

Retrospective cohort study using MIMIC-IV (Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care) v2.2 [15], which contains de-identified electronic health record data for patients admitted to the Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center between 2008 and 2019. The database was approved by the institutional review boards of the Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center and the Massachusetts Institute of Technology, and the requirement for informed consent was waived. We additionally used MIMIC-CXR [16], which provides radiology reports and structured CheXpert labels for chest radiographs.

### 2.2. Study population

Adults (≥18 years) meeting Sepsis-3 criteria [17] were identified from the derived `sepsis3` table. We retained the first sepsis-associated ICU stay for each patient; patients whose earliest ICU admission was for another reason were not excluded if a later admission met sepsis criteria, so the estimand is the first ICU episode in which sepsis was present, not the first ICU episode of any kind. The suspected infection time had to fall within 24 h before or after ICU admission, so that hospital-acquired sepsis arising later in the stay was not represented. A chest radiograph between 24 h before and 24 h after ICU admission was required, and when several were available the one closest to ICU admission was used.

Patients were excluded at six sequential steps. (i) No admission weight. (ii) Radiograph reports that did not permit classification of congestion status because the lungs were never described. (iii) Fluid balance not calculable because either the intake or the output record was entirely absent, or because the ICU stay ended before the 72-h window after the index film could be observed, so that the film-anchored balance was not calculable. (iv) Implausible values: admission weight below 20 kg or above 300 kg, or a 72-h fluid balance exceeding 20 L in absolute terms or lying outside −100 to +300 mL/kg. (v) Heart transplantation or ventricular assist device, identified from diagnosis and procedure codes, because structural ventricular disease alters both radiographic interpretation and fluid handling. (vi) Suspected infection falling outside the 24-h window around ICU admission. The plausibility thresholds were specified a priori on physiological grounds rather than derived from the observed distribution. Over a 72-h window anchored to the film, a charted balance outside −100 to +300 mL/kg would require net removal or retention of more than about 7 L or 21 L in a 70-kg adult, which charted ICU intake and output cannot produce. An absolute balance beyond 20 L is excluded for the same reason, and admission weights below 20 kg or above 300 kg lie outside the range in which weight-normalised balance is meaningful. The numbers removed at these steps are reported with the cohort flow.

### 2.3. Exposure: radiographic pulmonary congestion

Because imaging study identifiers are not carried in the ICU tables, chest radiographs were linked to ICU stays by subject_id together with the chart time of the film, keeping films inside the eligibility window. The index film was usually taken shortly before ICU admission; its timing relative to admission is described in Section 3.1. No film followed admission by more than 24 h. Each eligible study was matched to the radiology report with the closest chart time within ±2 h, restricted to reports whose text contained "chest" or "thorax" and excluding reports of computed tomography or magnetic resonance imaging. This restriction was introduced after an initial audit showed that unrestricted matching selected same-day CT reports in 3 of 20 sampled cases.

The impression section was extracted and classified in a fixed hierarchy. Explicit mention of oedema without negation was positive. Explicit negation such as "no oedema", or a statement that the lungs were clear, was negative. Otherwise the findings section was used, and if that also did not describe the lungs the study was classified as unknown. Structured CheXpert labels [18] were used only to assign positivity when both the impression and the findings were silent about the lungs; a CheXpert label could never override an explicit textual negative. Studies classified as unknown were excluded from the primary analysis and examined separately.

Two pre-specified alternatives were analysed: a findings-first definition in which any unnegated mention of oedema counted as positive, and a strict definition restricted to the impression section alone. Throughout, "radiographic congestion" denotes the content of the radiology report, not a direct measurement of extravascular lung water.

### 2.4. Exposure: fluid balance

Cumulative fluid balance over the 72 h after the index film was computed as the sum of all charted inputs in millilitres (excluding records flagged as rewritten) minus the sum of all charted outputs in millilitres, divided by the admission weight. The 72-h window was specified before analysis and was not selected from a scan of alternative windows. Intake and output are charted only during the ICU stay; for films taken before ICU admission the window therefore began at ICU admission, so a short interval between the film and admission was not covered (Section 3.1); for films taken close to discharge the window extended beyond the end of the stay. Truncation removes pre-ICU intake and output; because pre-ICU care usually includes fluid loading, the net effect is likely to make the observed balance more negative and to move borderline patients into group C. Pre-ICU intake and output were not available. Intake includes crystalloid, colloid, blood products, drug diluents and nutrition; output excludes insensible losses and is therefore systematically lower than physiological loss, so the measure is a charted rather than a physiological balance. Patients were classified as having positive balance if the value exceeded zero. For one sensitivity analysis we additionally required complete output recording, defined as a total recorded output of at least 0.3 mL/kg/h over the 72-h window with at least one output record, a threshold chosen because implausibly low recorded output indicates incomplete charting rather than true oliguria.

### 2.5. Cross-classification

Patients were assigned to one of four groups: A, no radiographic congestion and non-positive balance (concordant negative); B, no radiographic congestion with positive balance; C, radiographic congestion with non-positive balance (discordant congestion); D, radiographic congestion with positive balance (concordant positive). A balance of exactly zero was assigned to the non-positive groups (A and C). Throughout, “discordant congestion” denotes this cross-classification (group C) only; it is not a synonym for the agreement statistic, which is reported separately in Section 3.2.

### 2.6. Outcomes

The primary analysis concerned agreement between the index radiograph and the subsequent charted balance. The principal clinical outcome was 30-day all-cause mortality, defined from the date of death recorded in the hospital record or from the state death registry, and analysed as a secondary comparison across the four groups. In-hospital mortality was a further verification outcome because it does not depend on registry linkage. Additional outcomes were 90-day mortality, ICU length of stay and ventilator-free days.

### 2.7. Covariates

The following covariates were included: age, sex, the Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score [19] at the time of suspected infection, maximum lactate between 6 h before and 24 h after ICU admission from laboratory records (itemid 50813), invasive ventilation within 24 h, vasopressor use within 24 h, and history of heart failure and chronic kidney disease. The Charlson comorbidity index was not included in the primary model: in preliminary models, adjusting for the Charlson index alongside its own components produced coefficient instability in the heart failure and renal disease terms, consistent with collinearity, and the index embeds age, which is adjusted for separately.

### 2.8. Statistical analysis

Agreement between the radiograph and the subsequent balance was quantified with the 2×2 table and Cohen's κ. Fluid balance distributions were compared between congestion strata descriptively.

Agreement was quantified first and constituted the primary analysis. The secondary analysis was a single multivariable logistic model containing the four-level exposure; the overall effect was tested with a joint Wald test of the three indicator terms (3 degrees of freedom). Odds ratios are reported relative to group A. The contrast between groups C and A was pre-specified as the key comparison within that model but was recognised a priori to be the least powered. With 830 events and eleven parameters, the primary model had approximately 76 events per variable.

Lactate was defined as the maximum value between 6 h before and 24 h after ICU admission from laboratory records (itemid 50813) and was available for 88.3% of the cohort. Missing values (11.7%) were handled by multiple imputation with chained equations (m=20), including the outcome, all covariates and the group indicators in the imputation model; estimates were combined using Rubin's rules. All other covariates were complete. Discrimination was assessed by the change in the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), with a bootstrap 95% confidence interval (500 resamples), and by the integrated discrimination improvement. A difference in AUC below 0.01 was pre-specified as the threshold below which discrimination would be described as unchanged. The primary discrimination analysis added the binary congestion term to a model containing a natural cubic spline for 72-h balance; the four-level exposure was analysed secondarily in a model without a continuous balance term.

Three model specifications differing only in their comorbidity terms are reported in Table S1. Ten sensitivity analyses were performed. Eight were pre-specified: findings-first definition; 72-h landmark analysis; exclusion of patients with heart failure; restriction to frontal radiographs; a 24-h fluid window; exclusion of patients with incomplete output recording; in-hospital mortality; and the strict impression-only definition. Two were added after the analysis plan was fixed: a lactate hierarchy substituting the nearest available value for missing admission values, and a post hoc temporal comparison. The temporal comparison is reported separately for films taken before ICU admission, where the 72-h window is truncated, and for films taken at or after admission, where it is not. In addition, we examined the source of the positive classification within the discordant group (impression versus findings versus structured-label backfill). Two-sided p values below 0.05 were considered significant.

Four secondary analyses examined the shape of the association, its modification by congestion, its timing, and its dependence on the magnitude of the balance. First, a natural cubic spline (restricted cubic spline) with 4 degrees of freedom was compared with a linear term by likelihood ratio test. Second, interaction between congestion and fluid balance was tested both with a linear product term and with a spline-by-congestion product. Third, an increment model was fitted containing separate spline terms for the first-24-h balance and for the balance accrued between 24 and 72 h, each assessed by a joint Wald test. Fourth, the four-group model was refitted with the continuous 72-h balance added, to estimate how much of each group contrast is attributable to the magnitude of the balance. These analyses were not pre-specified and are interpreted accordingly. Loop diuretic administration was extracted from ICU input events (furosemide itemids 221794 and 228340; bumetanide 229639) and, for torsemide, from the hospital administration record (`emar`), because torsemide is not represented in the ICU item dictionary. The window ran from the later of the film and ICU admission to 72 h after the film, matching the balance window; ICU administration records cannot precede ICU admission, and hospital-recorded torsemide was restricted to the same interval. Administration means any recorded dose after the film, not necessarily a new start. A secondary count used the whole 72 h after ICU admission. Analyses used R 4.5.2. The study is reported in accordance with the STROBE statement [20].

During the preparation of this work the authors used an AI assistant to draft analysis code and to edit language. The authors reviewed and verified all code, re-ran every analysis reported here, and take full responsibility for the content of the manuscript.

### 2.9. Sample size

With 830 deaths and a 33.7% prevalence of radiographic congestion, the study had 80% power at α=0.05 to detect an odds ratio of approximately 1.20 for the overall congestion contrast. For the comparison of group C (n=493) with group A (n=602), the minimum detectable odds ratio was approximately 1.6. The overall four-group test was therefore designated as the principal inferential target for the mortality comparison, with the C-versus-A contrast reported as a pre-specified but power-limited comparison. The secondary comparison of group C adjusted for continuous balance (Section 3.8) was not part of this calculation and has less power than the primary contrast.

## 3. Results

### 3.1. Cohort

Of 32,971 ICU admissions meeting Sepsis-3 criteria, 25,596 were first sepsis-associated admissions in adults. A chest radiograph within ±24 h was available in 7,050, of which 6,595 could be matched to a chest radiograph report, 4,576 could be classified for congestion status and 4,341 had calculable fluid balance. After a plausibility screen (n = 174: admission weight 7, implausible fluid balance 155, ventricular assist device or transplantation 12; leaving 4,167 patients) and exclusion of 223 patients whose suspected infection fell outside ±24 h of ICU admission, the final cohort comprised 3,944 patients (Figure 1), contributing 830 deaths within 30 days (21.0%). The index film was taken a median of 1.4 h before ICU admission (interquartile range −3.5 to +1.7); 2,464 films (62.5%) preceded admission and 1,480 were taken at or after it, and for the former a median of 1.4 h (90th percentile 5.7 h) of pre-ICU time was not covered by the balance window.

Baseline characteristics are shown in Table 1. Group C differed from group A in age (median 72 vs 67 years; standardised mean difference 0.28) and in the prevalence of heart failure (62.1% vs 32.9%; SMD 0.61). It remained similar in acute severity: SOFA (SMD 0.18), vasopressor use (SMD 0.09) and lactate (median 2.1 vs 2.1 mmol/L; SMD −0.05). Median 72-h fluid balance was +26.5 mL/kg and differed by group (−14.4 mL/kg in A, +48.7 in B, −21.1 in C and +40.0 in D; Table 1).

### 3.2. Agreement between the radiograph and the subsequent balance

The two assessments agreed less often than chance would predict — 36.5% observed against 42.8% expected (κ = −0.109, 95% CI −0.136 to −0.083; Figure 2). Because a positive balance was common (72.2%), prevalence imbalance pulls κ towards zero and therefore understates the disagreement; the prevalence-adjusted, bias-adjusted κ was −0.27, and positive and negative agreement were 0.40 and 0.32 respectively, so the low agreement is not an artefact of prevalence alone (Table S2). Of 1,331 patients with radiographic congestion, 838 (63.0%) had a positive balance and 493 (37.0%) did not; conversely, 2,011 of 2,613 patients without radiographic congestion (77.0%) had a positive balance. Patients with radiographic congestion had a **lower** median fluid balance than those without (13.8 vs 32.6 mL/kg).

### 3.3. Mortality

Thirty-day mortality was 10.5% in group A, 20.0% in group B, 18.3% in group C and 32.8% in group D (Figure 3). After adjustment, the odds ratios relative to group A were 1.90 (95% CI 1.42–2.56) for group B, 1.75 (1.22–2.52) for group C and 3.27 (2.39–4.48) for group D; the joint test across the three indicators was significant (p < 0.001; Table 2). The crude difference in 30-day mortality between groups C and A was 7.8 percentage points (95% CI 3.6 to 12.0; 18.3% vs 10.5%). Lactate (OR 1.22 per mmol/L, 1.18–1.26), age (1.03 per year, 1.03–1.04), SOFA (1.08 per point, 1.02–1.13) and invasive ventilation (1.30, 1.09–1.56) were independently associated with mortality; heart failure (0.93, 0.76–1.12) and chronic kidney disease (1.06, 0.87–1.29) were not.

### 3.4. Secondary outcomes

Ninety-day mortality was 16.4% in group A, 26.6% in B, 25.2% in C and 39.4% in D; after adjustment with the same covariates as the primary model, the odds ratios relative to A were 1.70 (95% CI 1.33–2.18), 1.49 (1.09–2.04) and 2.62 (2.00–3.43). Median ICU length of stay was 2.3 days in A, 2.6 in B, 2.8 in C and 3.4 in D. Length of stay and ventilator-free days were examined unadjusted; the 90-day odds ratios were adjusted as described above.

### 3.5. Sensitivity analyses

The estimate for discordant congestion was 1.40–2.64 across the ten sensitivity analyses. It was 1.74 with the findings-first definition, 1.69 in the 72-h landmark analysis, 1.71 when restricted to frontal radiographs, 2.17 under the strict impression-only definition, 1.75 with the lactate fallback hierarchy, and 2.64 when patients with heart failure were excluded (Figure 4, Table S3). The 72-h landmark estimate is conditional on survival to 72 h and is therefore not directly comparable with the primary estimate. Two results attenuated the estimate to the boundary of significance: with a 24-h window it fell to 1.43 (1.00–2.05), and in the 2,464 patients whose film preceded admission, where the window is truncated, it was 1.40 (0.87–2.25). In the complementary 1,480 patients whose film was taken at or after admission, where the window is not truncated, it was 2.27 (1.27–4.04). A post hoc interaction test between the four groups and truncation did not detect heterogeneity across the two strata (χ² = 2.29, df = 3, p = 0.52), so the difference between these estimates is compatible with imprecision as well as with a genuine difference. With the 24-h window the positive-balance strata also lost most of their association (group B 1.15, 0.86–1.54). Two interpretations are possible: within the first 24 h the balance may not yet have reached the range in which accumulation becomes harmful, or a 24-h window may be too noisy to separate the strata. The analyses in Section 3.8 bear on this distinction, but neither interpretation can be established from these data. Restricting to patients with complete output recording gave 1.55 (1.06–2.27; n=3,229), and using in-hospital mortality gave 1.86 (1.17–2.95; 640 events).

Because the discordant group could in principle contain reports in which oedema appeared only in a differential diagnosis, we examined the source of the positive classification. Of the 493 patients in group C, 435 (88.2%) were classified from an explicit impression statement, and the remainder from the findings section or a structured label. Restricting the definition to impression-level statements gave a higher estimate (2.17, 1.36–3.47; n=2,526 under the strict definition, which requires an explicit impression statement and permits no structured-label backfill). The association did not depend on the looser textual definitions.

Missing lactate (460 patients, 11.7%) was handled by multiple imputation. Substituting the nearest available value between 24 h before and 72 h after admission for missing admission values gave an essentially identical estimate (1.75, 1.22–2.51).

Patients whose radiographs were unclassifiable (1,729 after the other screens) had an intermediate crude mortality (22.4%), between that of groups C and D. They were excluded from the primary analysis because their exposure cannot be classified; absence of a description of the lungs is therefore not a marker of low risk, and retaining them in the reference group would dilute the contrast.

### 3.6. Discrimination

Discrimination was assessed in two steps. In the primary analysis, congestion was added to a model that already contained age, sex, SOFA, lactate, ventilation, vasopressors, comorbidity and a natural cubic spline for 72-h fluid balance. The AUC rose from 0.7321 to 0.7403 (ΔAUC 0.0083, 95% CI 0.0015–0.0159), a point estimate below the pre-specified 0.01 threshold, although the upper confidence limit lies above it. In the secondary analysis, the four-level cross-classification was added to a model without a continuous balance term and raised the AUC from 0.7083 to 0.7256 (ΔAUC 0.0173). Because that exposure encodes both the radiographic finding and the direction of the balance, its increment is attributable to the classification as a whole rather than to the radiograph alone. Complete-case estimates (n = 3,484) are reported because paired bootstrap intervals require a common sample; the discrimination analysis was not repeated under multiple imputation. Collinearity was not a concern (largest variance inflation factor 1.29), and calibration of the primary model was adequate (Hosmer–Lemeshow χ² = 3.83, df = 8, p = 0.87; complete cases).

### 3.7. Selection into the radiograph cohort

Patients who underwent a radiograph were similar to those who did not: standardised mean differences were below 0.10 for age, sex, SOFA, ventilation, vasopressor use, heart failure and chronic kidney disease, and 0.08 for 30-day mortality (21.8% vs 18.5%). A model predicting radiography achieved an AUC of only 0.53.

### 3.8. Functional form, interaction and time dependence

We examined whether the relation between fluid balance and mortality was linear, whether it differed by radiographic congestion, and how it depended on the length of the observation window. A natural cubic spline (equivalent to a restricted cubic spline; 4 degrees of freedom) did not improve on a linear term for 72-h balance (likelihood ratio χ² = 3.98, df = 3, p = 0.26). The linear estimate was 1.06 per 10 mL/kg (1.05–1.08; Figure 5). Over a 24-h window there was neither a gradient nor evidence of non-linearity (χ² = 3.47, df = 3, p = 0.33), and the linear estimate was 1.01 per 10 mL/kg (0.99–1.03, p = 0.23).

There was no evidence that the association differed by congestion status, whether balance was modelled linearly (interaction OR 0.999 per 10 mL/kg, 0.972–1.027; p = 0.95) or by spline (χ² = 1.23, df = 4; p = 0.87). An interaction observed in a preliminary analysis of the same database was not reproduced and is not interpreted.

To separate early from later accumulation we fitted a model containing spline terms for the first-24-h balance and for the increment accrued between 24 and 72 h. The two components are collinear by construction, since their sum is the 72-h balance, so the comparison is descriptive rather than a test of independent effects. With that caveat, the increment contributed more (χ² = 105.1, df = 4, p < 0.001, versus χ² = 11.1, df = 4, p = 0.025 for the first 24 h). Shortening the exposure window to 24 h attenuated the four-group estimate (group C 1.43, 1.00–2.05; Table S3). Because patients who die or leave the ICU before 72 h contribute incomplete balance data, this analysis is conditional on remaining in the ICU long enough for the later balance to be recorded; the 72-h landmark analysis (Table S3) addresses the same vulnerability from a different direction.

Finally, adding the continuous 72-h balance to the four-group model moved the estimates for group B (1.90 to 1.10) and group D (3.27 to 2.08) towards the null, whereas the estimate for group C was numerically unchanged (1.77, 1.23–2.54; Table S4). This contrast was not pre-specified and is less powered than the primary comparison (minimum detectable odds ratio ≈1.6), so it should not be read as demonstrating an effect independent of balance. It indicates only that the group C association is not explained by the magnitude of the balance in this model. In the same analyses, spline terms for balance improved discrimination over a linear term by 0.0020 (95% CI −0.0012 to 0.0051), and a binary congestion term added 0.0083 (0.0015–0.0159) to a model that already contained a spline for balance, which is the primary discrimination estimate reported in Section 3.6.

### 3.9. Treatment after the radiograph

In a post hoc exploratory analysis, loop diuretics were recorded after the index film and within the same 72-h window used for the balance. The proportions were 225 of 602 patients in group A (37.4%), 385 of 2,011 in group B (19.1%), 339 of 493 in group C (68.8%) and 301 of 838 in group D (35.9%). Patients with a congested film and a non-positive balance were therefore the most heavily diuresed group, which is consistent with the negative balance in group C partly reflecting treatment given after the film rather than a measurement that disagreed with it. Because a recorded dose need not be a new start, and because dose and timing were not analysed, this cannot establish that treatment caused the negative balance; the untreated patients in group C still had a negative median balance. A broader definition — any administration within 72 h of ICU admission rather than within the balance window — gave 40.5%, 19.4%, 70.8% and 36.5%, the same ordering. Recorded administration was therefore more common after a congested film, which is consistent with, but does not prove, treatment following the film. The excess was not explained by case mix: after adjustment for age, sex, SOFA, lactate, ventilation, vasopressors, heart failure and chronic kidney disease, the odds of receiving a loop diuretic after the film were 2.84 (95% CI 2.16–3.72) in group C relative to group A. Within group C, treated patients had a more negative 72-h balance than untreated patients (median −27.8 versus −8.9 mL/kg), whereas in group B the two were nearly identical (48.0 versus 48.8 mL/kg).

## 4. Discussion

In 3,944 patients with sepsis, the index radiograph and the subsequent charted balance agreed less often than chance would predict (κ = −0.11, 95% CI −0.14 to −0.08). Nearly one in three patients with radiographic congestion had a non-positive fluid balance. Each measure was associated with 30-day mortality, although the estimate for the discordant group was attenuated to the boundary of significance in one sensitivity analysis. Throughout, group C is defined operationally as a report that mentioned pulmonary oedema while the measured balance was non-positive; it is not a direct measurement of extravascular lung water, and the term "discordant congestion" is used in that operational sense only. Radiographic congestion improved discrimination only marginally (ΔAUC 0.0083).

### 4.1. Comparison with previous work

Two small studies previously addressed related questions and reached broadly negative conclusions. The INCIVEX study found that cumulative fluid balance correlated with neither clinical oedema nor a comprehensive ultrasound congestion assessment in 50 patients. Our result is consistent in direction with the descriptive component of that finding in a cohort 79 times larger (our κ, a different metric from their correlation coefficients, was −0.11), and extends it by showing that the two measures are not equivalent markers of risk.

Mund and colleagues reported that a composite definition of cardiopulmonary congestion — clinical oedema, radiographic congestion, echocardiographic abnormalities and natriuretic peptide — was present in 78.4% of 102 patients with septic acute kidney injury and was unrelated to in-hospital survival, dialysis or renal recovery. Our results differ in direction, and several differences may explain this. Their exposure was a composite in which any one of several abnormalities qualified, producing a prevalence of 78% that suggests a low threshold; ours required a radiology report to describe pulmonary oedema, with a prevalence of 33.7%. Their cohort was restricted to acute kidney injury, in which fluid accumulation and outcome are already closely related, and their analysis did not report multivariable adjustment. Our study is approximately 39 times larger but is not directly comparable in population, and we do not claim to refute their finding; rather, the two studies suggest that the prognostic value of congestion depends on how it is defined and in whom it is measured.

### 4.2. Interpretation

Two mechanisms may explain the discordance, and treating disagreement between two measurements as itself informative has precedent in critical care [21]. In sepsis, pulmonary oedema often reflects increased capillary permeability rather than hydrostatic load, and may therefore occur without net fluid accumulation. This is consistent with our observation that patients with radiographic congestion but a non-positive balance were older and far more likely to have heart failure (62.1% vs 32.9%), yet had lower lactate than those with a positive balance. That pattern suggests a process not primarily driven by hypoperfusion. The relative contributions of cardiogenic and permeability mechanisms cannot be separated within this measurement framework. The association persisted, and was if anything stronger, when patients with heart failure were excluded. That exclusion changes both the population and the confounding structure, so the result shows only that the association is not confined to patients with heart failure; it does not by itself separate cardiogenic from permeability mechanisms, neither of which was measured. Whether congestion identified on imaging should prompt active fluid removal is now addressed in guidance on the de-escalation phase [22,23], but no trial has selected patients by radiographic rather than ultrasound criteria. Two caveats follow from the operational definition. First, group C necessarily mixes patients with true permeability oedema that the cumulative balance failed to capture with patients in whom the report mentioned oedema only as a differential diagnosis. The analysis of classification source in the Results shows that 88% of group C arose from an explicit impression statement, and that restricting to those cases gave a similar or higher estimate, which limits but does not eliminate this concern. Second, because the radiograph is an imperfect proxy for extravascular lung water, the observed prevalence of discordance may differ from the true one in either direction: misclassification of radiographs and under-recording of output can create apparent discordance as readily as they can mask it.

The small discrimination increment should be read alongside the association rather than as a separate null result. Radiographic congestion was associated with mortality after adjustment, which means it carries information; but that information is largely redundant with what the fluid chart, lactate and the SOFA score already convey. For a test that is obtained in most patients in the intensive care unit, the absence of incremental discrimination is worth stating plainly, because it cautions against using the radiograph as a risk-stratification tool in place of existing scores.

The analyses of functional form and timing qualify this picture in three ways. The excess risk associated with a positive balance was largely accounted for by the magnitude of the balance itself (group D 3.27 to 2.08). By contrast, the estimate for discordant congestion was numerically unchanged when balance entered as a continuous term (1.77, 1.23–2.54), a comparison that was not pre-specified and is power-limited. The prognostic signal arose chiefly from fluid accrued between 24 and 72 h rather than from the admission-day balance. And because a spline did not improve on a linear term, neither the null interaction nor the four-group contrasts can be attributed to forcing a linear dose–response onto a curved relation.

The first of these observations is the most consequential for interpretation. A positive balance and a positive radiograph are not interchangeable markers of the same process. Much of the excess risk of group D is the risk of having received a large volume of fluid, whereas the estimate for group C was not attenuated when the magnitude of the balance entered the model. That comparison was not pre-specified and is power-limited, so it does not establish an effect independent of balance; it does mean that the group C association is not simply a restatement of how much fluid was given. Two explanations are compatible with that pattern. One is a permeability component that the fluid chart cannot see. The other is a de-resuscitation phenotype, and the data are compatible with it. Active fluid removal is an established strategy in septic shock [24,25], and early diuretics are used for this purpose in septic patients with left ventricular dysfunction [26]. Patients in group C were more than twice as likely to receive loop diuretics after the film (68.8% versus 37.4% in group A), so much of their negative balance is plausibly a consequence of treatment given after the film rather than an independent property of the patient. We cannot separate the two retrospectively, and the observation argues against reading the radiograph as a proxy for cumulative balance rather than for a particular mechanism.

### 4.3. Strengths and limitations

The study has several strengths. The cohort is large, the exposure definitions were fixed before analysis and were checked against a manual review of 40 randomly selected reports, and the primary window was pre-specified. That review was a consistency check rather than a reliability study; per-report adjudications were not retained, so no agreement statistic can be reported for it. Ten sensitivity analyses were reported in full, including the two results that attenuated the main finding (the 24-h window and the truncated-balance subgroup). Selection into the radiograph cohort was small but not absent: standardised differences were below 0.10 for all covariates, although photographed patients had a 3.3 percentage-point higher crude mortality (21.8% vs 18.5%).

The limitations are equally important.

First, this is a single-centre retrospective cohort without external validation; the eICU database contains no structured echocardiographic or radiographic congestion measures. Reports that never described the lungs — 30% of patients with a matched report — could not be classified; their mortality was intermediate between groups C and D, so the exclusion is unlikely to have created the contrast. The analysed cohort represents 15.4% of the eligible sepsis admissions (3,944 of 25,596), and the findings apply only to patients in whom a radiograph was obtained, could be matched and classified, and had calculable balance.

Second, congestion was classified from report text and structured labels rather than from the images, and no imaging or ultrasound gold standard was available. Structured labels could only assign positivity when both text sections were silent, which makes the exposure asymmetric in principle. Among the 3,153 reports that never described the lungs, the label was positive in 42 (1.3%) and negative in 97 (3.1%), so reclassifying the negative-labelled reports could not plausibly explain the observed discordance. Because those labels derive from report text, silence is not evidence that the lungs were clear, and we did not treat it as such. The report is an imperfect measure of extravascular lung water [27,28] and no validated radiographic score such as RALE was used [29], with limited interobserver agreement [30]; misclassification is likely non-differential, biasing estimates towards the null, and a film obtained because deterioration was suspected describes a selected moment that cannot be separated from the exposure.

Third, the association depended on the 72-h window. With a 24-h window the estimate for discordant congestion was 1.43 (1.00–2.05), and the increment model — although compatible with a time-dependent interpretation (χ² = 105.1 versus 11.1) — is conditional on remaining in the ICU long enough for the later balance to be recorded. In the 2,464 patients whose film preceded admission the window is truncated (group C 1.40, 0.87–2.25); in the 1,480 whose film was taken at or after admission it is not (2.27, 1.27–4.04), and a post hoc interaction test did not detect heterogeneity (p = 0.52). Because truncation removes pre-ICU intake and probably moves borderline patients into group C, the truncated subgroup is not the cleaner test of the association, and we do not read its result as evidence against it.

Fourth, the analyses of functional form, interaction and timing were performed after the primary analysis and are exploratory; their p values are uncorrected. The group C contrast adjusted for continuous balance is a single secondary comparison at the margin of power (1.77, 1.23–2.54). Documented limitations of treatment were uncommon — a do-not-resuscitate or do-not-intubate (DNR/DNI) order within 24 h in 183 patients (4.6%) — and adjusting for them moved the estimate only slightly (1.69, 1.18–2.44); Table S5. Patients in group C were older and more often had heart failure, so a de-resuscitation phenotype and differential opportunity to be observed could still contribute; residual confounding by indication cannot be excluded, because unmeasured variables such as the trajectory of shock may contribute. The outcome relies partly on registry linkage; in-hospital mortality, which does not, was directionally consistent (1.86, 1.17–2.95).

Multiplicity is a further consideration. The primary inferential anchor is the joint test across the four groups, which does not depend on any single contrast; the C-versus-A comparison was pre-specified and its p value of 0.002 lies below the Bonferroni threshold for the ten analyses we report (0.05/10 ≈ 0.005). We therefore regard the discordant group as a consistent and pre-specified signal rather than a confirmatory finding, and we have not applied formal correction across sensitivity analyses.

---

## 5. Conclusions

In this cohort, a congested radiograph report and the subsequent charted balance were not interchangeable descriptions of congestion: they agreed less often than chance, and a congested film was usually followed by loop diuretic treatment, which plausibly explains much of the discrepancy. Congestion carried prognostic information beyond case mix, but the C-versus-A contrast was power-limited, and adding congestion to a model that already contained the balance, lactate and SOFA improved discrimination only marginally; whether that association is independent of the magnitude of the balance was not established, particularly in the 62.5% of patients whose window was truncated. The radiograph report should therefore not replace existing risk scores or the fluid chart; it is best read as a descriptor of phenotype and as a prompt to ask why a patient is congested when the chart says they are not.
